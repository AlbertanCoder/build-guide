What you're building & why

A 5-inch drone that carries a thermal camera, a GPS and a small computer, and puts the hot spots it sees on a map. You buy it in 7 stages and build it in 8 steps.

Details: what is on board and what is tested

The drone is a 5-inch analog FPV quadcopter carrying a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 WH, a Pimoroni MLX90640 thermal board and a PA1010D GPS. Separate sensor cables let the thermal lens point down while the GPS antenna points up. The flight stack supplies regulated 5 V 3 A for the Pi; the video transmitter runs from the flight controller's 9V pad.

The payload software groups hotspots, finds their position, logs them and shows them on an offline map. Its 31 synthetic tests passed. Fit, power, detection accuracy and flight still have to be proven by the tests in the build steps.

What it can and cannot see (the honest capability line)

Design target, not a verified capability: test a controlled warm target from low altitude and record detection rate, false positives and geolocation error. At 30 m AGL, a 55°×35° frame is about 31×20 m, or roughly 1×0.8 m per pixel; a 1 m target may occupy about one pixel and cannot be promised detectable. Day/night, emissivity, atmospheric conditions, motion, tilt and terrain all affect results.

Cannot: replace agency aircraft or declare an area safe. The MLX90640's 32×24 grid is a learning-grade sensor. No detection range, mapping accuracy, professional sensor cost, flight endurance or operational readiness claim is bench-verified for this build.

The one legal line that never bends: no drone may fly over or within 5 nautical miles (9.3 km) of a wildfire below 3,000 ft (CAR 601.15). Real fires are agency airspace — unauthorised drones ground air tankers and endanger crews. You will train on a safe heat target: a mug of hot water, never a fire.

One pipeline, six steps. 🌡 What would the thermal camera see?

SIMULATION, not a measurement

This shows what the 32 × 24 pixel thermal camera would make of a 1 m heat source, seen from straight above. The higher the drone, the more ground each pixel covers. When the fire is smaller than one pixel, that pixel shows a blend of fire and cold ground, so it reads much cooler than the fire really is.

0 °C300 °C

Each square is one pixel. A white outline marks pixels at 60 °C or more, the level the payload's detector looks for.

What this leaves out
  • Smoke and steam between the fire and the camera.
  • Wind, flames moving, flicker.
  • How well each surface gives off heat (every surface is treated as a perfect emitter).
  • Sun-heated rocks, roofs, roads or vehicles.
  • Lens blur. The real pixel edges are soft, which spreads and lowers a small hot spot further.
  • Drone tilt, height error and movement during the half-second a frame takes.
  • Trees, grass or anything covering the fire.
  • Real fires: a campfire is not a flat 1 m disc at one temperature.
Where the numbers come from
  • Burning campfire: Fraser, Choo, Kimble and Sutch, “The morning after the night before: campfires revisited”, Medical Journal of Australia 2003. 510 °C is the lowest value they give. The 1 m size is a choice made for this simulation.
  • Buried campfire: the same study measured a mean of 91 °C after 8 hours, through a small hole in the sand. The sand's own surface is probably cooler (not measured), so treat this as a stand-in.
  • Ground at 10 °C and the infrared band of the sensor are assumptions. Sensor noise of 0.35 °C comes from the Melexis MLX90640 datasheet, with one step of my own estimate for the payload's 2 frames a second.
  • Ground covered per pixel uses the same maths as the calculator above.
🔥 What "detecting a fire" actually means

The payload pipeline has three software stages, followed by hardware validation:

  1. Sense: the MLX90640 returns 768 temperatures per frame. A fire is a cluster of hot pixels — say 38 °C background, 300+ °C at the target.
  2. Detect: the included hotspots.py flood-fills edge-connected hot pixels into groups with centroid, size and peak temperature. Synthetic tests cover grouping, thresholds, edges and invalid inputs.
  3. Report: the included geo.py converts pixels using position, altitude and heading; payload.py logs results and map_server.py displays them offline. Detection and coordinate accuracy still require controlled hardware tests.

The same three layers work from the ground, the air, or a satellite feed — that's why the pipeline is the career asset, not the airframe.

                    2. Learn three things the multimeter does. Try each once, and write down what you see.

                      1. Put on your safety glasses.
                      2. Open a window and switch a fan on. Put the iron in its stand on a heat-proof surface. Use your ceramic tile or metal baking tray. A damp kitchen sponge wipes the tip.
                      3. Plug the iron in and wait for it to heat. When the iron arrives, check: how long it takes to heat and how to set its temperature. (The listing says 220 to 400 C.) Write it down.
                      4. Touch a little solder to the tip. It should melt at once.
                      5. Tinning means melting a small bead of solder onto a bare copper pad. Tin a few pads on the practice board. When the boards arrive, check: that the pads take solder on the side you use (the listing says double-sided; pad size and hole spacing are not stated).

                      2. Strip 3 cm of the outer jacket. Inside are four wires: two thicker ones carry power, two thinner ones carry data.
                      3. Find which thick wire is + and which is -. Set the multimeter to its lowest resistance (ohms) setting. Touch one probe to a thick wire and the other to the outermost pin at each end of the micro-USB plug. Close to 0 ohms means connected. Write down which wire is + and what the meter read.                       When the cable arrives, check: the wire colours inside, and which pin is pin 1 and pin 5 inside the plug.

                    6B · Solder the pins, fit the HAT, lengthen the GPS cable

                    1. Solder the straight male 1x5 header onto each sensor board. (A header is a row of metal pins.) Keep the right-angle socket header spare.

                      Line up the rows of pins. Press down gently and evenly until it sits flat. Do not wiggle it.

                      When the HAT arrives, check: which way round it faces.

                    3. Lengthen the GPS cable. (The thermal camera's cable is long enough as it is.)

                      1. Cut 4 pieces of silicone wire, 12 cm each. Strip about 5 mm off each end of each piece.

                      When the meter arrives, check: does it have a beep setting? Look for a sound-wave symbol next to the dial. If it does not, use its lowest resistance setting instead.

                    Stopping point: the sensor pins and the cable are done and checked with the meter.

                      The GPS needs to be outdoors with a clear view of the sky to get a position, but it answers at 10 indoors too.

                      When the Pi is running, check: the i2cdetect table against a screenshot from the real Pi.

                    Stopping point: Step 6 is done when both sensors answer.

                    Curious what the camera would see from different heights? Open the thermal preview.

                    2. Look at the battery. Dents, punctures or swelling: do not charge or fly it again. Put it in the LiPo bag. Do not throw it in the household bin: ask a recycling facility. When the charger arrives, check: the M7AC shows each cell's voltage while it charges (its first screen; its manual).
                    3. Props: replace any that are chipped, bent or white at the hub. Spares are on the buy list.
                    4. Motors, props off: spin each one by hand. A periodic scraping means a bent bell. After the next flight, a motor much hotter than the others points to a bent shaft or bearing.
                    5. Arms and frame: hold them to a light and look for hairline cracks, especially where the arms meet the plates.
                    6. Legs: straight, set screws tight, all four feet on. The drone must still stand level.
                    7. Solder joints: look at and gently tug the battery lead, the capacitor and the motor wires. Re-solder any that move or crack.
                    8. Video antenna: the nut is tight and not bent. The two zip ties still hold it.
                    9. Payload: the joined zip ties are not cut or slipped, the thermal camera still sits flat on its tape, the GPS board is still stuck down, and the GPS cable joints are not pulled.
                    10. Battery strap: not cut, and the pack has not slid.
                    11. Sensors: check again that the heat test finds the mug and not your hand (Step 8, item 9) and that the GPS line appears (item 10), before you fly the payload again.
                    12. Then a short low hover, then a normal flight.

                    ⚠ Safety: never near a wildfire — the law says 5 nautical miles (9.3 km). Only controlled targets on private land with permission. Read the Before you fly card first.

mlx = adafruit_mlx90640.MLX90640(i2c)

frame = [0] * 768
while True:
    try:
        mlx.getFrame(frame)
    except ValueError:
        continue
    for h in find_hotspots(frame, 60, 3):   # threshold 60C, 3+ pixels
