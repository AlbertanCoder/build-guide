A 5-inch quadcopter you build yourself, carrying a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W + MLX90640 thermal camera. What it can and cannot see (the honest capability line)

Can: detect a campfire-sized target (0.5–1 m) from ~30 m altitude, map it with GPS coordinates, and report it — in daylight or at night. Spot a person or vehicle, find a smouldering stump, prove a "cold" fire line is actually cold.

Cannot: replace agency aircraft. Professional wildfire platforms carry 640×512 sensors (8× sharper) on $15,000+ airframes, fly for hours on certified missions, and are crewed by trained operators. The MLX90640's 32×24 grid is a learning-grade sensor — perfect for proving the pipeline, not for protecting a town. Saying that out loud is what makes everything else you claim believable.

The one legal line that never bends: no drone may fly over or within 5 nautical miles (9.3 km) of a wildfire below 3,000 ft (CAR 601.15). Real fires are agency airspace — unauthorised drones ground air tankers and endanger crews. You will train on safe heat targets: fire pits, burn barrels, hot plates — on private land, with permission. That is exactly how professionals calibrate their gear too.
Why a drone beats the ground station for your portfolio

The same core skill — detect heat, georeference it, report honestly — but demonstrated on the platform everyone imagines when you say "wildfire tech". In interviews, "I built a thermal drone that maps hotspots" opens doors that "I built a ground sensor" doesn't. And the FPInnovations research (funded by Alberta Agriculture & Forestry) is explicit: "the RPA platform is only one small part of the solution… until the firefighter on the ground receives a map of GPS-referenced hotspots, the mission is incomplete." That map-making is the software skill you'll own.

Formulas (verified, from your geo.py): ground width = 2 × altitude × tan(FOV/2). Pixels are 32 across. Converting a pixel to latitude/longitude uses ~111,320 m per degree of latitude; longitude degrees shrink by cos(latitude). Approximate by design — and your README will say so.

🔥 What "detecting a fire" actually means

Three layers, and only the third one is "the drone":

  1. Sense: the MLX90640 returns 768 temperatures per frame. A fire is a cluster of hot pixels — say 38 °C background, 300+ °C at the target.
  2. Detect: hotspots.py flood-fills touching hot pixels into one hotspot with a centre, size and peak temperature. Threshold and minimum size are your tuning knobs.
  3. Report: geo.py turns the pixel position into latitude/longitude; the page draws it on a map with the drone's footprint rectangle; an optional AI button writes a 5-line briefing from the data only.

The same three layers work from the ground, the air, or a satellite feed — that's why the pipeline is the career asset, not the airframe.

⏱ Where & when you may fly (verified TC rules)

Basic operations — what this build flies under (TC page updated 19 Mar 2026):

  • Drone under 25 kg (yours will be ~1 kg) · you must always see it (visual line of sight)
  • More than 30 m horizontally from any person
  • Uncontrolled airspace, more than 5.6 km from a certified airport, more than 1.9 km from a heliport
  • Register your drone (250 g+) and mark it · carry your Pilot Certificate — Basic Operations (free online exam, age 14+, never expires)
Night flying is legal (CAR 901.39, quoted verbatim): "No pilot shall operate a remotely piloted aircraft system at night unless the remotely piloted aircraft is equipped with lights that are sufficient to allow the aircraft to be visible to the pilot or a visual observer… and those lights are turned on." — a $10 LED kit makes your drone legal for exactly the missions that matter.
The wildfire rule that never bends (CAR 601.15): never over or within 5 nautical miles (9.3 km) of a wildfire below 3,000 ft — fines to $25,000 and imprisonment possible. Before any summer flight, check for smoke plumes and local fire bans. Your test targets: fire pits, burn barrels, hot plates — on private land, with permission, fire bans checked, water/extinguisher on hand.

Buying notes & the two-battery rule
  • Canada shops: RotorVillage, Great Hobbies, Drone Dynamics, EpicFPV — or SpeedyBee/Motion RC direct (add shipping + possible duty).
  • The Pi + camera are the payload — they're identical to the ground-station build's parts, so if you already ordered them, tick them off.
  • Soldering iron required for the airframe (motor wires → ESC). A $30 adjustable iron + rosin-core solder is fine. The payload itself is solder-free.
  • LiPo safety: never charge unattended, charge in a LiPo bag, never fly a swollen or damaged pack. This is the one part of the hobby that genuinely bites.

Payload code — tested API, adapted for the Pi

import time, board, busio
import adafruit_mlx90640
from hotspots import find_hotspots   # your tested flood fill

i2c = busio.I2C(board.SCL, board.SDA, frequency=800000)
mlx = adafruit_mlx90640.MLX90640(i2c)
mlx.refresh_rate = adafruit_mlx90640.RefreshRate.REFRESH_2_HZ

frame = [0] * 768
while True:
    try:
        mlx.getFrame(frame)
    except ValueError:
        continue
    for h in find_hotspots(frame, 60, 3):   # threshold 60C, 3+ pixels
        print("HOTSPOT,%.1f,%.1f,%d,%.1f" %
              (h["x"], h["y"], h["pixels"], h["max_c"]))
    time.sleep(0.5)

Same library API as the verified ground code (getFrame, 768 values) — but on real Linux Python, no serial bridge. The flood-fill module is the one already passing 9/9 tests.
