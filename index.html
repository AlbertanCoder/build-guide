Build 2 — Winter concrete monitor

Waterproof temperature probes sit in poured concrete while an Alberta winter tries to freeze it. The system logs temperature for weeks, adds up how much curing has happened, and estimates strength — then you check it against a lab crushing your actual cylinders. This is an established method: the maturity method (ASTM C1074). Concrete gains strength through a chemical reaction that runs faster when warm and slower when cold, so temperature history can be turned into a strength estimate — once a lab has calibrated it for your exact mix.

  • Input: up to 6 waterproof DS18B20 probes (±0.5 °C from −10 to 85 °C; rated −55 to 125 °C) on one shared wire.
  • Math: curing total = (temperature − stopping temperature) × hours. Below the stopping temperature, time adds nothing.
  • Truth: a lab crushes cylinders of your exact mix at set ages; those results become the strength table. The system interpolates inside that table and refuses to guess outside it.
What this is not: a learning prototype, not a certified system. Never use it to decide when to strip forms or load real concrete. Even certified systems need calibration for each mix. The formula also cannot see frost damage — if a probe reads at or below 0 °C, the estimate stops being trustworthy.
Real-world note: the maturity method has been studied on exactly this kind of job — a 2007 study ran it on an industrial job in Edmonton, where live data helped decide when to add heat and protection.

Where's the drone? The rules and the research

Short answer: the law keeps this build on the ground, and the research says that's where your value is. Here is the verified evidence — then the upgrade path if you ever do go airborne.

The legal wall (verified: Transport Canada, 30 Oct 2026)

CAR 601.15 / CASA 2021-11: “No person shall operate an aircraft, including drones, over a wildfire or any area within 5 nautical miles (9.3 km) of a wildfire at an altitude of less than 3,000 feet above ground level.” Unauthorized drones force air tankers and helicopters to ground operations — and put lives at risk. Fines reach $5,000 for individuals and $25,000 for corporations, plus possible imprisonment for endangering aircraft. You must also “stay away from emergency sites such as traffic accidents and wildfires.”

So no hobby build can legally demonstrate on a real fire. The ground station isn't a compromise — for you, it's the only legal way to work this exact data.

What the research says (FPInnovations 2020 — funded by Alberta Agriculture & Forestry)

The federal-provincial research on exactly this question: “Remotely Piloted Aircraft Systems: Evaluation of Platforms for Hotspot Detection on Wildfires” (Technical Report No. 29). Its findings:

  • Drones are real and used — but for patrol and mop-up (finding smouldering hotspots after the fire is controlled), flown at night, because daytime sun creates false positives.
  • Real mission specs: tactical scans = night, low altitude; strategic scans = 100,000+ ha, 4–6 hour endurance at 5,000–10,000 ft. Pro platforms run $15,000+; prosumer drones only get 25–30 minutes per battery.
  • Night remote work needs paperwork: beyond-visual-line-of-sight operations require a Special Flight Operations Certificate from Transport Canada.
  • The killer quote: “The RPA platform is only one small part of the solution… until this data have been interpreted and the firefighter on the ground receives a map of GPS-referenced hotspots, the mission is incomplete.” That map is exactly what Build 1 already makes.
  • No proven cost win over helicopters — the review found no cost-effectiveness comparison favouring drones; helicopters remain the preferred tactical platform.

What Alberta actually does — and what a real thermal drone costs

Alberta Wildfire (Budget 2024 announcement, verified): $151 million over three years for wildfire preparedness — including additional drones for aerial wildfire surveillance, drone and helicopter testing by Alberta Wildfire personnel, and night-vision helicopters (goggles amplify light ~60,000×) for overnight bucketing. Alberta has run an AI wildfire-occurrence prediction system since 2022. The 2023 season: 1,094 fires, 2.2 million hectares. It's real, it's funded — and it's agency and contractor work.

import time
import board
import busio
import adafruit_mlx90640

# The STEMMA QT socket on this board is board.STEMMA_I2C() (SDA1/SCL1).
# Adafruit's example uses board.I2C(), which is the *other* pins on this board.

mlx.refresh_rate = adafruit_mlx90640.RefreshRate.REFRESH_2_HZ

frame = [0] * 768            # 32 x 24 = 768 temperatures per frame
while True:
    try:
        mlx.getFrame(frame)
    except ValueError:
        continue             # busy frames happen; just retry
    print("FRAME," + ",".join("%.2f" % t for t in frame))
    time.sleep(0.5)

Build 2 — wiring the concrete probes (no soldering)

  1. Plug a STEMMA QT cable into the QT Py's small socket, other end into the DS2484 adapter.
  2. Connect each probe's wires to the adapter's 3 screw terminals. All probes share the same 3 screws — that's the whole point of 1-Wire.
  3. Install CircuitPython on the QT Py (use the build matching your board on circuitpython.org).
  4. Copy adafruit_ds248x.mpy and the adafruit_bus_device folder into CIRCUITPY/lib.
  5. Copy the code below to CIRCUITPY/code.py and open a serial monitor.

The adapter's 3 screws, left to right (verified: Adafruit's pinouts page)

V+Red probe wire (3–5 V)
1WYellow or white — data
GNDBlue or black (4-wire version: black)

Adafruit's probe comes in two wire styles — check the colours on the ones you get against this list and Adafruit's guide before power-on. On the 4-wire version, red = 3–5 V, black = ground, white = data, and the bare copper wire is the shield.

The small resistor in the probe bag: the 4.7k pull-up is required when wiring a probe straight to a bare microcontroller pin. Adafruit's DS2484 adapter board is designed to run the bus directly — keep the resistor in the bag, and add it between V+ and 1W only if the code finds no sensors.

Longer wires

Each probe has 91 cm of cable. To reach the board from outdoors, join probes to 3-wire cable with lever connectors. Keep the board warm and dry indoors; leave only probes and cable outside. Test your full cable length on the bench before you pour — no tested length limit is published for this exact setup.

Build 2 board code — every call verified against Adafruit's library source

import time
import board
from adafruit_ds248x import Adafruit_DS248x

# board.STEMMA_I2C() is the small socket. Adafruit's example uses
# board.I2C(), which is a different set of pins on this board.
bus = Adafruit_DS248x(board.STEMMA_I2C())

# Every probe has its own serial number. Find them all once.
probes = []
bus.onewire_search_reset()
while True:
    serial_number = bytearray(8)
    if not bus.onewire_search(serial_number):
        break
    probes.append(serial_number)
print("FOUND,", len(probes))

# Each probe takes about 3/4 of a second to read.
while True:
    readings = [bus.ds18b20_temperature(p) for p in probes]
    print("TEMPS," + ",".join("%.2f" % t for t in readings))
    time.sleep(60)

Verified 30 Oct 2026 against the published adafruit_ds248x.py source: constructor takes an I2C bus (default address 0x18); onewire_search_reset(), onewire_search(new_addr) (returns True while devices remain) and ds18b20_temperature(rom) all exist with exactly these signatures, and the read includes the 0.75 s conversion wait. Not yet run on real hardware — that is the Week 5 bench test.

Prove the probes before you trust them

  1. Put every probe in a cup of crushed ice and a little water, and stir. Good probes read close to 0 °C.
  2. Hold each probe in your hand, one at a time, to learn which serial number is which. Label each cable.
  3. Write down each probe's ice reading. That's your calibration record.

Pouring safely

  • Wear waterproof gloves and eye protection. Wet cement can burn skin — wash it off right away.
  • Wear a proper dust mask when opening and mixing bags; mix outdoors or with good airflow.
  • Keep power and the board away from water. Only the probes go in the concrete.
  • Never add anything to a real job without written approval from the person in charge.
One number to settle with the lab: what strength protects concrete from freezing. US sources say 3.5 MPa; two Canadian websites quote 7 MPa from CSA A23.1, a paid standard this guide could not read. So this guide has no built-in pass mark — ask the lab, or use your project's own spec.

  • Checked Wages and outlook — Job Bank Canada (NOC 21211, Alberta): low $28.00/hr, median $43.27/hr, high $57.69/hr; outlook Moderate for 2025–2027.
  • Checked The maturity formula and its limits, against NRMCA CIP 39, Nebraska DOT's maturity procedure, and an FHWA technical brief. ASTM C1074 is the standard name for the method.

Still to prove (your tests, or a phone call)

  • To prove That the whole sensor chain works on your parts. Week 5 tests it. The board code is Adafruit's documented pattern but has not yet run on your hardware.
  • To prove That the board can log for weeks with your laptop off. Week 6. If it can't, the laptop stays on and awake during pours.
  • To prove Which lab takes your cylinders, and the price. Parkland and Inline list concrete testing in Edmonton, but publish no prices and no sign they serve individuals.
  • To prove What strength protects concrete from freezing here: US sources say 3.5 MPa; Canadian sites quote 7 MPa from CSA A23.1, a paid standard this guide couldn't read. Ask the lab.
  • To prove Canadian prices and stock on the day you buy. Test ages (suggest 1, 3, 7, 28 days) must be agreed with the lab.
  • Re-check Any job posting before relying on it — the GoA numbers quoted in the Q&A came from live postings during the week of 30 Oct 2026 and they close. Job Bank wages get updated yearly.
