Would this impress people?

More than a tutorial certificate, less than production experience — and the gap closes the moment you show the honest parts. Anyone can demo what works. People lean in for a lab comparison chart showing where you were wrong, passing tests, an honest-limits README, and explaining every file without notes. Most juniors can't do that last part even with a degree.

How hard is it, really?

The code is the easy part — small pure-logic modules tested on a laptop. The hard parts: (1) wiring that must pass an ice test before you trust it, (2) concrete logistics — a lab crushes cylinders on fixed dates you can't miss, (3) honest self-review — a discipline, not a skill. Physically beginner-friendly: no soldering, click-in cables, screw terminals.

What exactly am I building — the three faces

1 — Wildfire field station: thermal camera finds hotspots, maps them next to real CWFIS satellite data, AI writes a crew briefing. 2 — Winter concrete monitor: probes log concrete temperature; the maturity method (ASTM C1074) estimates strength — checked against real lab crushes. 3 — Wildfire data pipeline: free Alberta open data + daily hotspots, cleaned, documented, charted on a public dashboard. Same core skills — sensors/data in, tested logic, map/chart out, honest reporting — three employers' vocabularies.

❄️ Build 2 — Winter concrete monitor

Waterproof probes sit in poured concrete while an Alberta winter tries to freeze it. The maturity method (ASTM C1074) turns temperature history into a strength estimate — then a lab crushes your actual cylinders to check it. The reaction hardening concrete runs faster warm, slower cold, so temperature history tracks curing — once calibrated for your exact mix.

  • Input: up to 6 DS18B20 probes (±0.5 °C from −10 to 85 °C) on one shared wire
  • Math: curing total = (temp − datum) × hours
  • Truth: lab crushes → strength table; the code refuses to guess outside the tested range
Not a certified system — never use it to decide when to strip forms or load real concrete. The formula can't see frost damage: if a probe reads ≤ 0 °C, the estimate stops being trustworthy. A 2007 study ran this method on an industrial job in Edmonton.

🛩️ Where's the drone? The rules & the research

Short answer: the law keeps this build on the ground — and the research says that's where your value is.

CAR 601.15 / CASA 2021-11 (verified): no aircraft — including drones — may operate over a wildfire or within 5 nautical miles (9.3 km) of one below 3,000 ft. Unauthorized drones ground firefighting aircraft. Fines to $5,000 (individuals) / $25,000 (corporations) plus possible imprisonment.

FPInnovations research (funded by Alberta Agriculture & Forestry): drones do patrol & mop-up at night — finding smouldering hotspots after the fire is controlled. Pro platforms $15,000+, 20–50 min endurance, night beyond-visual-line-of-sight needs an SFOC. And the killer line: “the RPA platform is only one small part of the solution… until the firefighter on the ground receives a map of GPS-referenced hotspots, the mission is incomplete.” That map is what Build 1 already makes.

Build 1 board code — API verified against Adafruit's libraries 
import time
import board
import adafruit_mlx90640

# The STEMMA QT socket on this board is board.STEMMA_I2C() (SDA1/SCL1).
# Adafruit's example uses board.I2C(), which is the *other* pins here.
i2c = board.STEMMA_I2C()
mlx = adafruit_mlx90640.MLX90640(i2c)
mlx.refresh_rate = adafruit_mlx90640.RefreshRate.REFRESH_2_HZ

frame = [0] * 768            # 32 x 24 = 768 temperatures per frame
while True:
    try:
        mlx.getFrame(frame)
    except ValueError:
        continue             # busy frames happen; just retry
    print("FRAME," + ",".join("%.2f" % t for t in frame))
    time.sleep(0.5)

Adafruit's own simpletest pattern with the FRAME, print your SerialCamera reader expects. Still has to pass on your real parts — the Week 5 test.

Build 2 — wiring the probes (no soldering) + board code
  1. STEMMA QT cable: QT Py small socket ↔ DS2484 adapter.
  2. All probes share the adapter's 3 screws — that's the point of 1-Wire.
  3. Install CircuitPython; copy adafruit_ds248x.mpy + adafruit_bus_device into CIRCUITPY/lib.
  4. Copy the code below to CIRCUITPY/code.py, open a serial monitor.
V+Red (3–5 V)
1WYellow or white — data
GNDBlue or black (4-wire: black)

4-wire version: red = 3–5 V, black = GND, white = data, bare copper = shield. The bag's 4.7k resistor is for wiring straight to a bare pin — keep it in the bag; add it between V+ and 1W only if the code finds no sensors. Board stays warm and dry indoors; test your full cable length on the bench before any pour.

import time
import board
from adafruit_ds248x import Adafruit_DS248x

bus = Adafruit_DS248x(board.STEMMA_I2C())

probes = []
bus.onewire_search_reset()
while True:
    serial_number = bytearray(8)
    if not bus.onewire_search(serial_number):
        break
    probes.append(serial_number)
print("FOUND,", len(probes))

# Each probe takes about 3/4 of a second to read.
while True:
    readings = [bus.ds18b20_temperature(p) for p in probes]
    print("TEMPS," + ",".join("%.2f" % t for t in readings))
    time.sleep(60)

Every call verified against the published adafruit_ds248x source (constructor takes an I2C bus, default 0x18). Not yet run on real hardware — that's the Week 5 bench test.

Prove the probes & pour safely
  • Ice test: every probe in crushed ice + a little water, stirred. Good probes read near 0 °C. Hold each in hand to learn which serial is which cable; label them; write down each ice reading — your calibration record.
  • Safety: waterproof gloves + eye protection (wet cement burns skin); dust mask when mixing; board and power stay away from water; nothing goes on a real job without written approval.
One number to settle with the lab: what strength protects concrete from freezing. US sources say 3.5 MPa; Canadian sites quote 7 MPa from a paid standard this guide couldn't read — so there's no built-in pass mark. Ask the lab.

The cover-letter formula & your 30-second pitch

Address the education line in one confident sentence:

"I offer equivalent, demonstrated experience in place of formal education: production-grade data systems I designed, built, tested and demoed, documented at [portfolio URL], with references who can speak to the work. The Alberta Public Service's Minimum Recruitment Standards recognize such equivalencies."

Then spend the letter on evidence: specific numbers, specific tools, specific outcomes.

"I come from concrete work on Alberta jobsites, and I'm moving into tech. I built a low-cost sensor system that estimates concrete strength in the cold, and checked it against lab results. It was off by [your real number]. The same system also maps wildfire hotspots. I can explain every part of it. Want to see?"

Fill in your real number. Never say it until you've measured it.

Earning a vouch & the 3-minute demo structure
  1. Show, don't ask. First ask: “can I show you this for 3 minutes?” never “can you get me a job?”
  2. Pick people who know the work — superintendent, lab tech, Tech Wednesdays regular, AI lead.
  3. Ask for criticism: “What would make this useless on a real site?” Then fix what they say.
  4. Go back with the fix. That's what turns critics into supporters.
  5. Then ask plainly:
"You've seen how I worked on this, including what went wrong. Would you be comfortable being a reference for me? It's completely fine if not."

Demo flow: 0:00 the problem · 0:30 the machine (heat frame → hotspots → map → satellite comparison) · 2:15 the AI briefing · 2:45 the honest limits, first: “not certified, approximate position, 32×24 — a prototype, and the pros already do this. But it runs, and every line is tested.” Saying that first is what makes the rest believable.
